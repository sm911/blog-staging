If your MSP’s weekly report is a wall of “Critical” CVEs and nobody agrees which ones matter by Friday, you do not have a tooling problem. You have a risk-strategy problem — and that lives in NIST CSF 2.0’s Govern function.

The scanner-noise problem

Most SMBs I support in the Hudson Valley — professional services, healthcare practices, manufacturers, community institutions — already scan something. Vulnerability tools are cheap; attention is not. Without an agreed risk appetite for patching, every finding looks urgent, so nothing is. Boards ask “are we patched?” and get a percentage that hides internet-facing KEV exposures sitting past any reasonable SLA.

NIST’s Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 puts risk management strategy under Govern (GV.RM): determine risk appetite and risk tolerance, and use them to prioritize cybersecurity activities.3 The CSF 2.0 Resource & Overview Guide and SMB Quick Start material reinforce the same idea for smaller organizations: start with outcomes and ownership, not a shopping list of controls.4

SMB trigger

Endless vuln-scanner noise, no written remediation SLA with your MSP, and a business owner who only hears about patching after a vendor incident headline — that is the moment to borrow BOD 26-04’s prioritization logic.

What BOD 26-04 actually requires

On June 10, 2026, CISA issued Binding Operational Directive 26-04: Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk. Federal civilian executive branch agencies must prioritize remediation using a risk model that weighs public exposure, Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog status, exploit automation, and technical impact — with remediation timelines that can be as short as three days plus forensic triage in the worst cases.1

CISA’s companion implementation guidance walks through how agencies should operationalize that model and when forensic triage is expected.2 You are almost certainly not a federal agency. Treat the BOD as a free prioritization curriculum, not a compliance mandate.

Public exposure × KEV status × exploit automation × technical impact — four questions that beat “everything Critical is equal.”

Those four factors are the heart of the directive. Ask them in order:

  • Is the asset publicly exposed? Internet-facing beats internal every time for initial triage.
  • Is the CVE on CISA’s KEV catalog? Known exploitation in the wild collapses “theoretical” into “operational.”
  • Is exploit code automated or trivially weaponized? Mass scanning changes your calendar.
  • What is the technical impact if exploited? Remote code execution on an identity provider is not the same as a low-impact info leak on a brochure site.

Mapping to CSF 2.0: GV.RM, Identify, Protect

Here is how I map BOD 26-04 into CSF 2.0 language for SMB programs:

GV

Govern — GV.RM

Write risk appetite for patching: which classes of findings get day-level attention vs. weekly ranking. BOD timelines become your SLA language, adjusted for SMB capacity.

ID

Identify

You cannot answer “public exposure” without an inventory of internet-facing assets and the services they run. Tag the top 20 exposures first; perfection is optional.

PR

Protect — PR.PS / vuln mgmt

Platform security and vulnerability management execute the SLA: patch, configure, or mitigate (WAF virtual patch, remove exposure) within the agreed window.

DE

Detect (supporting)

KEV subscriptions and exploit-intel feeds are Detect inputs that keep Govern’s prioritization honest week to week.

Notice what this mapping does not do: it does not demand you adopt every federal reporting artifact. It demands that someone owns the risk questions and that Protect work follows that ownership. That is the Govern function CSF 2.0 added for a reason.3

NY relevance: Frontier AI and faster exploitation

For New York–anchored firms — including those watching NYDFS expectations even when Part 500 does not formally apply — the Department’s May 21, 2026 industry letter on heightened cybersecurity risks associated with frontier AI models is useful supporting context.5 The letter flags AI-accelerated vulnerability discovery and exploitation as a reason to treat patch velocity as a living risk issue, not an annual project.

I am intentionally not restating NYDFS’s separate September risk-assessment guidance here. The Frontier AI letter is the narrower, patching-relevant signal: assume adversaries find and weaponize flaws faster than your old “30-day for High” spreadsheet assumed.

A right-sized SMB remediation SLA

Federal three-day clocks with forensic triage are not always realistic for a 40-person firm. Right-size without watering down the logic:

Condition SMB target (example) CSF anchor
KEV + internet-facing Remediate or mitigate within 7 calendar days; document compensating control if patch blocked GV.RM PR
KEV + internal only Remediate within 14–30 days; confirm no unexpected exposure ID PR
Critical CVE, not KEV, exposed Risk-rank within 7 days; remediate per impact (often 14–30 days) GV.RM
Everything else Weekly ranked backlog; no silent infinite deferrals GV.RM

Put that table on one page. Add the CISA KEV catalog URL. Sign it with the business owner and the MSP lead. That signature is the Govern artifact.

Action this week

90-minute working session

Tag your top 20 internet-facing assets as exposed / not (VPN portals, mail, web apps, remote-access gateways, cloud consoles).

Subscribe to CISA KEV email alerts (or confirm your MSP already does and forwards actionable items).

Write the one-page SLA above; share it with your MSP and one business owner before Friday.
