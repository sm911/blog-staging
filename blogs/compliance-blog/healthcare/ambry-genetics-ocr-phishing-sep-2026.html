OCR’s $700K Ambry Genetics Settlement: Phishing Lessons for SMB Healthcare

A January 2020 phishing email still taught the same Security Rule lesson in September 2026 — and it is not only for genetics labs.

By Jim Venuto | September 22, 2026

If you run a dental, medical, behavioral-health, or telehealth practice — or you are a business associate that handles electronic protected health information (ePHI) — you do not need to be a national genetics company to care about OCR’s newest resolution.

On September 17, 2026, the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) announced a resolution with Ambry Genetics Corporation: a $700,000 payment and a two-year corrective action plan after a phishing compromise of an employee email account that may have exposed the protected health information of 225,370 individuals. The Resolution Agreement and Corrective Action Plan are public on HHS’s site.

I sit with Hudson Valley and regional practice owners when a phishing near-miss, an insurance questionnaire, or a vendor change forces the honest question: would our access hygiene and risk analysis survive the same OCR review? Ambry’s Covered Conduct is a blunt checklist — and it maps to work most small practices can start this week.

What OCR Said Happened

According to the Resolution Agreement, Ambry discovered on January 22, 2020 that an email account was compromised by a targeted phishing attack. OCR’s investigation indicated three Security Rule failures (the “Covered Conduct”):

1. Risk analysis. Failure to assess potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of ePHI (45 C.F.R. § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A)).
2. Termination procedures. Failure to implement procedures for terminating access to ePHI when employment ends or access is no longer appropriate (45 C.F.R. § 164.308(a)(3)(ii)(C)).
3. Unique user identification. Failure to assign a unique user name and/or number for identifying and tracking user identity in systems containing ePHI (45 C.F.R. § 164.312(a)(2)(i)).

The Agreement is not an admission of liability by Ambry. It is OCR’s public statement of what it found worth fixing — and what the CAP will monitor for two years: a thorough risk analysis with an asset inventory, a risk management plan, written policies (including email/PHI use), unique user IDs, and workforce training with certifications.

Why SMBs Should Care (Even If You Are Not Ambry)

  • Phishing is still the boring door. Email remains how invoices, lab results, prior-auth threads, and “updated W-9” scams arrive. OCR keeps saying so.
  • Risk analysis is still the foundation. Director-level remarks this year keep calling incomplete risk analysis a major concern. A stale PDF that ignores shared inboxes and departed contractors is a soft “no” for underwriters and investigators alike.
  • Access leftovers create quiet breaches. Former employees, temp schedulers, and “the old front-desk login” are how ePHI walks out after the phishing story ends.
  • Shared accounts break accountability. If two people share frontdesk@ on the EHR, you cannot track who opened what — and OCR called that out explicitly.

What I Want You to Do This Week

This week’s action: Pick one EHR / Microsoft 365 / imaging / billing system. Export active users. Mark every shared or generic account. Confirm every terminated workforce member from the last 90 days is disabled. Put the dated export in your compliance folder.
  1. Inventory who can still open ePHI — clinic staff, contractors, billing vendors, and the MSP admin accounts. Name an owner for the list.
  2. Kill shared logins — replace “office” / “temp” / shared EHR IDs with unique identities. Document exceptions with an expiration date.
  3. Test termination — run a tabletop: HR notifies IT the same day; MFA tokens and mailbox access die same day; EHR rights die same day; evidence is a ticket screenshot.
  4. Refresh the risk analysis — if you have not used HHS’s free SRA Tool (now at 3.7) since your last major stack change, schedule the working session. Tie phishing, email, and remote access into the scope.
  5. Train on your policies — not a generic YouTube module. CAP language keeps stressing organization-specific Security Rule training with attestation.
