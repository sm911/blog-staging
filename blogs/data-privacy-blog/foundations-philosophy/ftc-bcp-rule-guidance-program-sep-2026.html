When the Rule Is Fuzzy: FTC’s New BCP Rule Guidance Program for SMBs

On September 10, 2026, the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection opened a public channel for genuine rule ambiguities — not a free legal opinion on whether your privacy notice “looks fine.”

James Venuto · Security Medic Consulting, LLC - AI Cyber Security and Privacy · September 25, 2026 · 8 min read

Privacy and consumer-protection rules are written for millions of businesses. The edge cases still land on Main Street: a wellness app that shares data with advertisers, a loyalty program that changes pricing, a health-adjacent wearable that sits outside HIPAA. Owners ask a fair question — “What does the FTC actually expect?” — and get marketing blogs instead of answers.

On September 10, 2026, Christopher Mufarrige, Director of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection (BCP), announced a new BCP Rule Guidance Program. Stakeholders can submit questions about genuine ambiguities in Commission rules, substantive conflicts between a rule and an existing statute or other rule, and other significant issues inside Commission rules. If BCP decides a response is appropriate, it intends to publish the guidance publicly so everyone benefits — not just the asker.

I work with Hudson Valley and regional SMBs who cannot retain a 50-person privacy bench. This program is useful. It is also easy to misuse. Here is how I brief owners.

What the Program Is For

  • Flagging a real conflict between an FTC rule and another binding requirement.
  • Surfacing a genuine ambiguity that existing plain-language FTC guidance does not already answer.
  • Raising a significant structural problem in a rule — not a request to water down your obligations.

What Staff Say They Will Not Do

BCP’s announcement is blunt about the rejection pile:

  • Questions that simply ask staff to restate what the rule already says.
  • Questions that try to diminish a business’s own legal or compliance responsibilities.
  • Requests to interpret performance standards like “clear and conspicuous” — those have case-law meaning and must be applied by businesses and counsel.
  • Questions already answered by the FTC’s existing plain-language guidance for a rule.
Owner translation: This is not “please bless my privacy policy.” It is “here is a specific conflict or hole in the published rule set that affects how we comply — and if you answer, please answer for everyone.”

How Everyday SMBs Should Use the Moment

  1. Read the plain-language guidance first. Many FTC rules already have business-facing explainers. Start there before filing a form.
  2. Document the ambiguity in one page. Cite the rule text, the conflicting requirement (if any), and the concrete business decision you cannot make without clarity.
  3. Decide counsel vs. program. High-stakes enforcement exposure, health data, or children’s data usually still needs counsel. The program is a transparency channel, not a retainer.
  4. Watch for published answers. Because responses are meant to be public, a competitor’s well-framed question may unlock guidance you can use too.
  5. Keep your notices honest while you wait. Ambiguity is not permission to over-claim. CalPrivacy’s recent accuracy and registration actions show that wrong public statements still cost money.
One-sentence MSP / counsel ask: “Please review our top three consumer-facing data practices against current FTC plain-language guidance, flag any genuine rule ambiguities worth a BCP Rule Guidance Program submission, and confirm we are not treating ‘unclear’ as ‘optional.’”

Why This Matters Beyond Washington

The same week BCP launched this program, the Commission was also cleaning up obsolete sub-regulatory statements. The pattern for SMBs is consistent: published rules and plain-language guidance first, formal clarification channels second, marketing blogs last. Owners who can show a dated reading of primary sources fare better with customers, partners, and underwriters than owners who waited for a LinkedIn thread.
