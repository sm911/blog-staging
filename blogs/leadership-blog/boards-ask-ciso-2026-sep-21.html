What Boards Should Ask
Their CISO in 2026

Without a 40-slide deck. The 2026 NACD–ISA Director’s Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight pushes recurring CISO access, business-impact metrics, and decisions — not tool logos.

James Venuto  •  Security Medic Consulting, LLC - AI Cyber Security and Privacy  •  September 21, 2026  •  9 min read

Owners and boards at SMBs and mid-market firms still get cyber updates that sound like IT status reports — open tickets, patch counts, and a parade of tool logos. Someone asks if things look “okay.” The CISO or MSP says mostly yes. The packet advances. Nobody in the room has made a decision, accepted a residual risk, or connected a number to what would happen to payroll, patient scheduling, or client delivery if the wrong vendor failed on a Friday afternoon.

The 2026 NACD–ISA Director’s Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight — with a foreword from CISA — pushes the opposite posture. Cyber is strategic, financial, and operational risk. Boards need recurring, direct access to the CISO function. Reporting should emphasize business impact, clear escalation thresholds, and decisions the governing body can actually take — not an annual briefing that doubles as a product demo.

I work with Hudson Valley owners, community boards, and professional-services partners who do not have a full-time CISO on staff. The trigger is usually practical: a board packet due this quarter, a lender or cyber-insurer questionnaire, or a CEO asking “are we okay?” after a vendor-incident headline. This post is not another metrics taxonomy. It is a decision-oriented packet you can run in under thirty minutes — aligned with how NACD frames board–CISO relationships and cybersecurity board reporting in the 2026 toolkit.

If the board can read the cyber section and leave without making a decision or formally accepting a risk, the packet failed — no matter how polished the slides.

01

What NACD 2026 Is Asking Boards to Do Differently

The handbook’s core message is governance discipline, not tool selection. Boards should treat cyber risk with the same seriousness they apply to financial and operational risk: clear ownership, recurring oversight, and escalation paths that do not wait for the annual retreat. The companion toolkit materials on building a board–CISO relationship and on cybersecurity board reporting emphasize access, candor, and formats directors can use — not dashboards designed for security engineers.

For an SMB or mid-market firm, that does not mean copying a Fortune 500 cyber committee charter. It means three practical habits: the person who owns cyber risk (staff or fractional) gets direct time with the board or owners; the packet is short and decision-ready; and “green” is never accepted without a story about residual risk and what would change the color.

Old Ritual

Annual briefing, tool logos, patch percentages, and a nod. No decision request. No formal risk acceptance.

NACD Direction

Recurring CISO access, business-impact framing, escalation thresholds, cyber as strategic/financial/operational risk.

SMB Translation

Three numbers, one decision, one residual-risk note — every quarter. Owner or board can act in one meeting.

02

Questions Worth Asking (Not a 40-Slide Deck)

Replace the inventory of tools with questions that force translation into business outcomes. These map cleanly to how directors are being coached to oversee cyber risk in 2026:

Board / Owner Question Set

Access: When did we last speak directly with the person who owns cyber risk — not only the MSP account manager?
Impact: Which three systems, if unavailable for 72 hours, stop us from serving customers or meeting payroll?
Identity: What share of internet-facing and privileged access has MFA and monitored EDR — and which exceptions remain open?
Recover: When did we last restore from backup into a usable environment — date, scope, and who watched?
Exploit pressure: How many CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) or critical patches on exposed assets are past our SLA?
Vendors: Which third parties could quietly change what our customers see (CDN, forms, email) — and who reviews their incidents?
Decision: What do you need from us this quarter — budget, risk acceptance, policy change, or a tabletop date?

Notice what is missing: mean time to detect in isolation, phishing click rates without consequence language, and coverage percentages that never name the crown-jewel systems. Those can live in the operational appendix. The board packet needs the translation layer.

03

A One-Page Packet: Three Numbers + One Decision

Here is the format I recommend for the next quarterly packet — deliberately different from a metrics catalog. Put it on one page. Attach evidence in an appendix if someone wants to drill.

Signal Example board-ready number Why it matters
Protect % of internet-facing / privileged accounts with MFA + EDR (and named exceptions) Identity and endpoint failure is still how most SMB incidents start
Recover Date of last successful restore test (scope + observer) Untested backups are a story, not a control
Pressure Open KEVs / critical patches on exposed assets past SLA Forces prioritization instead of “we’re patching”
Ask One decision: accept risk, fund a fix, set a tabletop date, or change vendor terms Turns oversight into governance

This Week’s Action

Replace the next board cyber slide with three numbers (for example: percent of internet-facing assets with MFA + EDR; last restore-test date; open KEV / critical patches past SLA) plus one decision you need from the board — not a tool inventory.

  • Draft the one-pager before the meeting; do not invent metrics in the room.
  • Name the exceptions out loud — silent exceptions are unaccepted risk.
  • Record the decision (or formal risk acceptance) in the minutes.
04

Escalation Thresholds Beats Green Dashboards

NACD’s reporting guidance works when directors know when something must leave the operational channel. Agree in advance on a short list: ransomware indicators on a production system; loss of backup integrity; a critical vendor incident affecting customer-facing scripts; or KEVs on internet-facing systems past SLA. Those trigger an out-of-cycle note to the chair or owners — not a wait for the next quarterly logo parade.

Trade press summaries of 2026 board cyber recommendations land in the same place: lead with impact, demand clarity on residual risk, and keep the CISO relationship direct. Use those pieces as corroboration; use the NACD handbook and toolkit as the primary frame.

Governance is the accountability loop: decide, record, revisit. Observation without a decision is just consumption.

Schools and local governments can run the same packet with a board of education or town board. The systems differ — SIS, dispatch, tax collection — but the questions do not. Direct access to whoever owns cyber risk, three honest numbers, and one decision request still beat a forty-slide vendor deck.

If you do not have a staff CISO, the fractional model exists for exactly this gap: not to replace your MSP’s operational work, but to own the translation layer the board is now expected to demand.
